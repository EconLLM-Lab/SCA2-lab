Synthetic Cultural Agents as Measurement
Instruments: A Preregistered Evaluation from
Cross-National Anchor to Survey Respondents

Abstract
Cross-national surveys commit to items before learning whether they separate
countries; language models offer a cheap first reading. The usual route simulates
individuals, then averages. We ask the model only about the group. Six declared
signs of country-level survey moments label synthetic preference pairs that train
adapters, which read items without naming the country. On trust, they order
countries as the survey does on two scenario banks. A prompt stating those
signs does too, so signs carry the order. Against a retrospective criterion, a
preregistered screen of held-out items gives a weak signal that registered contrasts
cannot separate from questionnaire readers. Descriptively, the adapters’ order
does not detectably agree with respondents’, and their response distributions lie
farther from the survey than the base model’s. That is, the instrument orders
countries like the survey while answering unlike the people in them. It can
complement human pretesting. We have not tested pre-piloting.

Keywords: synthetic respondents; large language models; measurement validity;
survey item screening; preregistration; cross-national measurement.

Introduction

Cross-national survey programs commit to their items long before they learn how
those items behave. A question about trust must be translated, pretested and fielded
in many countries before a researcher sees whether its country differences follow the
construct it was written for. Language models offer a cheap first reading of such items
(Argyle et al., 2023; Horton et al., 2023; Buskirk et al., 2026). A reading that flagged
in advance which new items separate countries would be useful at exactly this stage.
We build one such reading and check, link by link, what it does against a benchmark
survey and against the respondents of a second survey.
The usual route to a synthetic reading goes through individuals. Silicon sampling
describes a person in the prompt, collects the model’s answer, and averages many
such persons into a country figure (Argyle et al., 2023). That route asks a language
model for two things it does poorly. First, it needs a model of each simulated person.
The errors of those simulated people are correlated, because one model with one set of
assumptions produces all of them. Averaging more of them therefore does not cancel
the bias. Second, it needs a sampling frame: someone must decide which people to
simulate and in what proportions. The record so far is cautionary. Synthetic responses
are sensitive to prompt wording, compress the variance of human answers and depend
on the order and labelling of answer options (Bisbee et al., 2024; Boelaert et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2025; Dominguez-Olmedo et al., 2024). Alignment procedures change
how faithfully models simulate attitudes (Lyman et al., 2025), and methodological
work in this journal concludes that validation against human data remains essential
(Kozlowski and Evans, 2025; Broska et al., 2025). Most empirical work asks whether a
model reproduces answers that people already gave, whether it is prompted (Santurkar
et al., 2023; Durmus et al., 2024) or fine-tuned on survey response distributions (Cao

2

et al., 2025; Suh et al., 2025). In the fine-tuned case the human survey sits inside the
training signal. Language-model pretesting has also been studied as flaw detection in
question wording (Sturgis et al., 2026). Whether a method can say which new items
will separate countries in an intended direction is a different test. It needs a decision
rule fixed before the human criterion is read.
We take a route that never models individuals. Cross-national research usually
wants a country-level quantity, such as how countries rank on generalized trust. A
probability survey such as the Global Preferences Survey (GPS; Falk et al., 2018) has
already aggregated people into such quantities, with sampling weights. We take its
country-level moments as declared inputs and ask the model only for a group-level
reading of new item text. In other words, the survey aggregates people, and the
language model reads items at the level of the group. An order of countries can survive
this design without a representative respondent. It requires only that the instrument’s
score rise with the group’s latent preference. The design has a price. A single choice
policy per country has no spread within the country built in, so it can carry an order
and has no reason to match the distribution of respondents’ answers. This is the
representative-agent problem (Kirman, 1992), and it gives a reason to expect that
order and distribution can come apart. Because country-level agreement says nothing
about individuals (Robinson, 1950), we chose the country as the unit and keep our
claims there.
Modelling the group directly is not new on its own. SubPOP (Suh et al., 2025)
and Group Preference Optimization (Zhao et al., 2023) predict group-level responses
without simulating individuals, but they train on human response distributions and
name the group. What is specific here is the combination of three features, and each
is a choice a researcher can inspect. First, the inputs are declared aggregate moments.
The researcher states which six GPS moments enter, and a sign rule turns them into
3

labels, so nothing about the conditioning hides in a country name or in human answers.
Second, the training data are synthetic preference pairs. Each country’s sign profile
labels one shared bank of generated response pairs. Direct Preference Optimization
(Rafailov et al., 2023), which we read as a regularized Bradley–Terry pairwise choice
model, then fits one adapter per profile. No human survey answer enters training.
Therefore, a human survey is a criterion the method has never seen, which is not true
of models fitted to response distributions. Third, the group is unnamed at evaluation.
Items are scored with prompts that never mention a country, so whatever the adapters
carry comes from the conditioning. A country-token channel is closed by construction.
We evaluate the construction along a chain of three links, treating each as a
separate measurement question in the way Wallach et al. (2025) propose for generative
systems. The anchor is the GPS, the instrument is the set of adapters, and the
respondents are the people who answered Wave 7 of the World Values Survey (WVS).
Prompted models have been compared with the GPS across countries and found to
be weak proxies for it (Chen et al., 2026). We built the construction on a purposive
development panel of 16 countries. We then evaluate it on all 42 countries covered by
both the GPS and Wave 7, with a second set of adapters for all 64 sign profiles on a
newly generated scenario bank. At the first link we ask whether training installs the
synthetic labels, whether the order of countries survives the new bank, and whether a
prompt does as well. A factorial over the 64 sign profiles asks whether the six declared
dimensions behave as separate inputs. At the second link we ran a preregistered
screen of 223 Wave 7 items that no adapter was built for. A probe that reads only
the adapters’ scores assigns each item a dimension and a direction, and a fixed rule
selects the strongest assignments before any human value is read. That criterion
is retrospective, because the survey existed before the probe. Finally, descriptive

4

analyses added after the registered results had been read set the adapters beside the
respondents’ composites and response distributions.
What we found on the first link is an order of countries that the declared signs
carry. Training installs each country’s synthetic labels on scenarios the adapters never
saw. On trust, the adapters keep the GPS order of countries when the scenario bank
is replaced, whereas a prompt that names the country does not. A prompt that states
the same six signs keeps the trust order as well, and the contrast between the adapters
and that prompt is unresolved. That is, the signs carry the order, and the evidence
gives the trained weights no separate credit. The order is specific to trust. Each
declared sign moves its own surface in the intended direction and also moves the
others, often by more. Trust is the one surface where its own sign dominates.
Checked against people, the instrument answers unlike them. The retrospective
screen gives a weak signal. The registered null is rejected, yet the signal is a small
fraction of what two human survey waves achieve on the same items, and its countrybootstrap interval reaches zero. Registered contrasts do not distinguish the adapters
from careful readers of the questionnaire on the few items they share, and no dimension
passes both the second-bank check and the screen. Descriptively, the adapters’ order
of countries does not detectably agree with the respondents’ composite on any surface.
Notably, the GPS’s own agreement with those respondents is loose. On response
distributions the ranking reverses. The adapters sit farther from the survey’s response
distributions than the base model does, whereas a country-named prompt sits closer
and has no trust order. The separation of order from distributional fidelity is known
(da Silva et al., 2026; Wang, 2026). What this case adds is one method, on one anchor,
that answers the two questions in opposite directions. Put plainly, a model asked
only about the group can order countries the way a cross-national survey does while
answering unlike the people in them.
5

Our contributions are three.
1. An inspectable group-level conditioning method. Declared aggregate
moments, synthetic preference pairs and an unnamed group at evaluation define
a construction whose information budget is on the record. A reader can see
what the method was given and check where its readings depart from people.
2. A reusable evaluation protocol. We separate the three links and state
what a favorable or unfavorable result at each permits. The protocol includes
a screen of new items whose probe is fixed before any human value is read,
and a permutation null that absorbs the probe’s selection rate. It adds a
human ceiling from two survey waves, item-matched comparators that read only
the questionnaire, and a factorial that tests whether declared dimensions stay
separate.
3. A specific empirical failure profile. The method preserves the labels and the
trust order, entangles the dimensions and screens new items weakly. It moves
away from respondents’ response distributions while a country-named prompt
moves toward them.
What the paper shows is bounded by its design: one anchor, one instrument family
and one base model. We do not show that trained weights improve on a prompt that
states the same signs. Nor do we show that the adapters agree with respondents
beyond the weak retrospective signal, or that screening would change which items
a researcher fields. Pre-piloting is a prospective use that remains untested. The
instrument carries no information about magnitudes or about variation among people
within a country, and its readings concern differences among countries. Within these
bounds a survey designer can consult it beside human pretesting as a complement,
never in its place.

2

Synthetic Respondents as Measurement Instruments

Synthetic respondents and the criterion they need. A language model conditioned on a description of a population can be asked to answer for a sample of its
members. In silicon sampling the description enters the prompt, and the model’s
response distribution is read as an estimate of the population’s (Argyle et al., 2023).
In the homo silicus program the model plays an economic agent in a stylized decision
problem (Horton et al., 2023). A systematic review maps how fast these uses have
spread across survey research (Buskirk et al., 2026). Fine-tuning on survey responses
is one response to the evidence on prompted models (Suh et al., 2025; Cao et al.,
2025; Krsteski et al., 2026). Two reviews in this journal draw the conclusion that
matters here: validation against human data remains essential (Kozlowski and Evans,
2025), and model predictions are best treated as potentially informative observations
while human subjects remain the gold standard (Broska et al., 2025). In other words,
both require a criterion against which the synthetic reading can fail. Our evaluation
supplies one criterion for each link of the chain.

Construct, measure, and anchor. Three objects have to be kept apart. A
construct is a theoretical preference attribute, such as generalized trust. A measure
operationalizes it through observed answers, such as the response distribution to a
7

WVS question about whether most people can be trusted. An anchor is an aggregate
score that conditions the instrument, such as a country’s GPS trust coordinate. The
GPS supplies anchors for six economic preferences: trust and risk taking, patience and
altruism, and positive and negative reciprocity. It measures them with experimentally
validated modules in a common design across countries (Falk et al., 2018). We treat
these coordinates as declared aggregate moments: the researcher chooses them, and
nothing in the construction certifies them as the right moments for a given survey.
Mapping a WVS item to a GPS dimension makes the item a candidate indicator,
and an average of such items a proposed composite. Agreement between instrument
and anchor, between instrument and people, and between anchor and people are
three different facts. They are the three comparisons that the chain makes. The
multitrait–multimethod logic of Campbell and Fiske (1959) applies directly. That is,
the human survey is the other method, and an instrument that agrees with its own
anchor has passed a construction check without yet meeting a criterion.

Cross-national comparability. We chose the country as the unit of analysis.
Agreement across countries says nothing on its own about individuals, the problem
of ecological inference (Robinson, 1950). Country-level agreement does not establish
that an item measures the same construct for individuals in different countries,
because loadings and intercepts can differ across groups in ways that leave country
means comparable for some purposes and misleading for others (Steenkamp and
Baumgartner, 1998; Davidov et al., 2014). Work in this journal argues that countrylevel composites can suffice for simple group comparisons despite noninvariance, while
understating uncertainty (Bratt, 2025). A simulation study in the same journal
finds that unaddressed measurement error and, less so, noninvariance bias multilevel

8

estimates (Pokropek et al., 2025). Every comparison with respondents below concerns
country rankings, with no claim about invariance or individual-level validity.

What is left open. Prior evaluations score models on questions whose human
answers are known, or fine-tune them on human survey responses. What remains
open is what an instrument built from declared aggregate inputs alone preserves of its
anchor and of the respondents’ answers. We ask that question link by link, with the
selection rule for new items registered before the human criterion is read.

3

The Instrument

The instrument is the middle link of the chain. It maps declared aggregate preference
signs from the anchor into choice policies and reads them on survey items without
naming any country. A language model is treated throughout as a conditional
distribution over response sequences given a prompt (Ludwig et al., 2026).

3.3

Two cohorts and two prompt routes

Two kinds of question need two sets of adapters: how countries compare and how
each sign acts. The first cohort has one adapter for each of the 42 countries in the
intersection of the GPS and WVS Wave 7, trained on the original scenario bank.
These countries realize 26 distinct sign profiles. The second cohort is trained on a
second, independently generated bank of 658 pairs under the same sign rule and text
restrictions. It contains one adapter for each of the 64 possible sign profiles, a full 26
factorial in the six sign bits. The labels depend on the country only through its sign
profile. Therefore, each country takes its profile’s adapter in country-level comparisons.
Most of the 64 profiles are design cells that no country realizes.
Adapters are one channel for the signs, so we also score two prompt routes. The
sign-profile prompt route gives the base model the same six signs in words, with no
country name and no adapter. It carries the same declared information through a
different channel and is the natural comparison for the trained route. The countrynamed persona route replaces the neutral opening of the evaluation prompt with
“Answer this questionnaire as a typical adult living in” followed by the country’s name,
and supplies no signs. It changes the information as well as the channel, because
the model can draw on whatever it associates with the country name. Naming the

11

population is the comparison a survey researcher would reach first, so we score it on
all 42 countries.

A construction check asks whether the adapters encode what they were trained on

4.1

Second-bank check

An order tied to one scenario text would say little about the signs. For each surface
of the strict battery we compute the Spearman correlation between the adapters’
surface composites and the GPS coordinate of the surface’s own dimension over
the 42 countries. We do this first for the first cohort (S1) and then for the second
cohort, with each country assigned its profile’s adapter (S2). Intervals come from
a profile-block bootstrap that resamples the 26 realized sign profiles. It uses 2,000
draws, seed 20261011 and percentile limits. The imprint set is the 4 dimensions whose
first-cohort interval excluded zero: trust, risk taking, positive reciprocity and altruism.
Patience and negative reciprocity were flat and are reported without interpretation.
The rule was fixed before the second cohort was trained: for an imprint dimension
whose second-bank interval includes zero, we write no sentence about carry-over to
the second bank. We also report the agreement between the two banks over the 26
shared profiles. It measures how much the scenario text changes the instrument and
involves no human data.

4.2

The country-named prompt

This route tests whether a country name alone carries the order. We scored the
persona route on the strict battery for each of the 42 countries in one run on the frozen
stack. We compute the persona–GPS correlation for each surface (P) and one planned
contrast, fixed in a design note committed before any persona was scored. That
contrast is the first cohort’s trust correlation minus the persona route’s, recomputed
on every draw of the profile-block bootstrap above. All other differences are descriptive.

4.3

The held-out screen

Item pool. The screen tests new items, so its design was fixed before any human
value was read. The pool was built from WVS Wave 7 metadata alone: ordinal
questions with two to eleven contiguous response codes outside the battery used to
develop the adapters. A text review excluded items with non-monotone codes or
factual content and repaired wording that did not extract. That leaves 223 items that
no adapter was built for (online supplement).

Probe. All 64 second-cohort adapters scored every pool item under the frozen
specification. For each item i, the probe regresses the 64 adapter scores on an
intercept, the six sign bits coded ±0.5 and their 15 two-way interactions. That leaves
42 error degrees of freedom. Let td be the main-effect t statistic of dimension d.
The item’s assigned dimension is di = arg maxd |td |, or none when the largest |td | is
below 2.0, and its sign is si = sign(bdi ). The primary rule keeps items whose assigned
dimension is in the imprint set and whose |t| is at least 2. It keeps at most the top 25
percent of all pool items by |t|, which caps the selection at 56 items. The secondary
rule keeps every imprint item above the threshold and is a reliability check only. The
probe reads model scores only. The permutation null absorbs the rate at which noise
items cross the threshold.

fixed in advance. If A is not above the null at one-sided α = 0.05, the adapters do not
screen better than random assignment. If A is above the null but below one third of
the human ceiling, a screening signal exists but is weak.

Comparators. A positive A could reflect item wording alone. Two comparators

each route’s own items beside it. The preregistration named in advance that both

Readout variants and the prompt route. A selection that moved with option
order or phrasing would reflect the prompt. The probe was therefore re-estimated
on three re-scorings of the pool (options reversed, two prompt paraphrases). The
selection was declared dependent on wording if more than half of the selected items
changed dimension or sign. The same probe and test were applied to the 64 prompted
profiles.

4.4

Registration, blinding and amendments

The screen was registered in a document committed to the project repository and
timestamped by a pushed git tag. That happened before any held-out item was scored
by an adapter, when no selection existed and no held-out human value had been read.
A freeze guard enforced the order of work (a timeline is in the online supplement).

17

We scored the held-out items, ran the probe and froze the hashes of the selection and
the item pool. Only then did we join the human data.
Two amendments were made, both before any human held-out value was read.
Amendment 1 repaired 12 items whose standalone wording could not be answered
as written, under the pool rule and override mechanism already registered. Every
route was then re-scored as one run. We disclose that the first probe pass had been
computed and read before the repairs were chosen, and that two repaired items (Q153
and Q157) were in its top quartile. The comparators had assigned the pool on the
pre-repair wording. Therefore, Amendment 2 removed the repaired items from both
comparator routes before any comparator statistic was computed. It affected one item
(Q82), which is outside the adapter selection. That decision preceded any human
value. The amendment text, written after the primary statistic was read, records it.
The criterion is retrospective. The Wave 7 file is public and existed before
the probe, the pool and the adapters. It is the only human source in this study.
Registration guards against tuning the probe to the criterion. It cannot make the
criterion prospective, so results on surface H are labelled retrospective throughout.
The online supplement discloses the provenance gaps, none of which touches a statistic
reported here.

4.5

The sign-profile factorial

The factorial asks whether the six declared signs act as separate inputs. The second
cohort covers all 64 sign profiles, so the strict-battery composites form a full factorial
in six binary factors. For each of the seven surfaces we fit the probe’s regression. That
is, a main effect is the difference between the mean composites of profiles with the bit
set and without it. Because the bits were assigned by design, this is a causal statement

18

about the instrument. It carries no implication about how preferences operate in
people. Each bit has an own surface (patience owns two). The analysis plan set one
acceptance check, separate from the held-out registration. At least 4 of the six bits
must move their own surface more than any other (V5), against a binomial reference
with a one-in-seven chance of a hit. Exploratory views divide effects by each surface’s
standard deviation over the 64 profiles and count the surfaces whose largest mover is
their own bit. They also report the first principal component’s variance share and the
mean absolute correlation between surfaces. The same analyses on the 64 prompted
profiles contrast two instruments that carry the same declared information.

4.6

Retraining noise and the near-zero margin

Effects can only be read against the noise of the pipeline itself. We retrained one
profile adapter without a fixed seed, which gives a noise floor. A predeclared sensitivity
check also removes the training pairs of every dimension whose GPS coordinate lies
within 0.10 of zero. It refits two countries (Great Britain and Colombia), each beside
a size-matched control. The rule was to report the margin and never to promote it.

4.7

Descriptive follow-up analyses

Four further analyses were added after the registered results had been read. Two of
them are agreement in three columns and distance to the survey’s response distributions.
The other two are a decomposition of the factorial’s cross effects and held-out label
recovery, both defined with their results. None is preregistered or tests a rule fixed
in advance. We label their numbers D and read them as descriptions. Their human
input is the Wave 7 file used for the screen, so every comparison with respondents is
retrospective.

Results

We report the results along the chain, and each statistic belongs to one surface of
Table 1. Sections 5.1 to 5.3 concern the link from anchor to instrument. They ask
what training installs, which order of countries survives a new scenario bank and what
carries it, and whether the declared dimensions stay separate. Sections 5.4 to 5.7
concern the link from instrument to respondents. They cover the preregistered screen
and its comparators, agreement with the respondents’ composites, and distance to their
response distributions. Everything computed against the Wave 7 file is retrospective.
Four figures carry the evidence beside the text that reads them. Exact values are in
the text, Table 2 and the online supplement.

5.1

Anchor to instrument: what training installs

Every training pair of a country carries a label. The chosen completion is the one
that goes with the sign of the country’s GPS coordinate on the pair’s own dimension.
The first question is whether that installation carries over to pairs the adapters never
saw. This is a construction check on the synthetic labels (descriptive, no human
data). We split the training pairs once into a training part and a held-out part that
the adapters never saw. For each of the 42 first-cohort adapters, the margin is the
difference between the log probability of the chosen and of the rejected completion.
We scored it under the adapter and under the base model in a further run on the
frozen stack (gates in the online supplement). Recovery is the share of held-out pairs
on which the adapter’s margin exceeds the base model’s. Accuracy is the share on
which the adapter prefers its own chosen completion outright, and base accuracy is the
same share for the base model. We report country-equal means with a profile-block
bootstrap.
21

Recovery is 0.940 [0.926, 0.952] and accuracy is 0.816 [0.771, 0.856]. The base
model’s accuracy is 0.489 [0.471, 0.506]. That interval includes one half, so the base
model shows no detectable preference for the chosen completions before training. The
mean margin shift is 40.3 [28.2, 54.1] nats. Training installs each country’s synthetic
labels on scenarios the adapter never saw, which is construction fidelity. It says
nothing about whether the labels order countries like the GPS, and nothing about
respondents. Notably, the distance results below show that the same adapters lie
farther from the survey’s response distributions than the base model does.

5.2

Anchor to instrument: the trust order and what carries
it

The first cohort encodes four of the six declared signs on the strict battery (S1). Over
the 42 countries, the adapter–GPS Spearman correlation is 0.744 [0.500, 0.865] for
trust and 0.435 [0.085, 0.686] for risk taking. It is 0.488 [0.120, 0.695] for positive
reciprocity and 0.406 [0.020, 0.676] for altruism. These four form the imprint set that
the screen later uses.
The second cohort tests whether the order survives a change of scenario text (S2).
Trust keeps its agreement with the GPS, at 0.803 [0.615, 0.876]. The other three
imprint dimensions keep positive point estimates, but their intervals now include zero
(Table 2). Therefore, 1 of the 4 imprint dimensions passes the second-bank check
and 3 do not. Under the rule fixed before the second cohort was trained, we write
no sentence about the other three carrying over. Trust is the dimension on which
retention of the anchor’s order is replicated across scenario banks. The banks also
agree profile by profile. Over the 26 shared profiles, the rank correlation between
first-bank and second-bank trust composites is 0.907 [0.774, 0.957]. Risk taking is

22

the least stable surface, at 0.544 [0.187, 0.772], which fits its loss on the second bank
(the online supplement gives every surface). This agreement measures how much the
scenario text changes the instrument and says nothing about people.
What carries the trust order? The comparison a survey researcher would reach
for first is a prompt that names the population. It does not reproduce the order
(P): the persona–GPS correlation over the 42 countries is 0.028 [−0.339, 0.381]. The
planned contrast with the first cohort is 0.716 [0.191, 1.154], and the reading is the
same under a country bootstrap. A prompt that states the same six signs in words,
with no adapter and no country name, tracks trust as closely as the adapters do. It
reaches 0.773 [0.584, 0.844] (panel A of Figure 4, which sets all routes side by side).
That is, on trust what separates the routes is whether the declared sign is supplied,
whichever channel carries it. The contrast sets declared signs against a country name
and says nothing about trained weights against prompting. On the other surfaces the
persona route’s intervals include zero, except for patience, which it orders positively
on one item and negatively on the other (online supplement).

5.3

Inside the instrument: the sign-profile factorial

The factorial asks whether the six declared signs behave as separate inputs (F). On the
trained route, every sign bit moves its own surface in the intended direction (panel A
of Figure 1; the online supplement gives each effect with its t statistic). All seven own
effects are positive, from 0.364 for trust (t = 16.74) to 0.124 for positive reciprocity
(t = 3.02), and 0 of 7 point the wrong way.
The planned separability check fails. Only 2 of the 6 bits move their own surface
more than any other surface, where at least 4 were required (binomial p = 0.207).
They are the bits for altruism and negative reciprocity. Scale-free views agree (online

23

supplement). Each bit moves its own surface and also moves others, often by more.
The risk-taking bit moves the altruism surface by 0.983, against the altruism bit’s own
effect of 0.242. The trust bit is the largest mover on 3 of the 7 surfaces. One common
direction carries 51.3% of the variance of the seven standardized surfaces, and the
mean absolute correlation between surfaces is 0.395. In other words, each sign keeps
its intended direction on its own surface, while the size of its effects spreads across
the others.
The prompted route fails the check by the same count, 2 of 6 (binomial p = 0.207),
so V5 does not separate the routes. What separates them is the common direction
and the sign of the own effects. Under prompting the first principal component carries
83.5% of the variance, the mean absolute correlation between surfaces is 0.807, and
the trust bit is the largest mover on all 7 surfaces. Three prompted own effects point
against the intended direction. Two are risk taking at −0.457 (t = −22.31) and
patience Q43 at −0.069 (t = −19.75). The third, negative reciprocity at −0.023, is
not distinguishable from zero at t = −1.80. These are behavioral cross effects of the
instrument. The contrast concerns entanglement and the direction of own effects and
is silent on screening accuracy, where the difference between routes is unresolved.

5.5

Instrument to respondents: questionnaire readers and
the prompt route

The registered contrasts do not distinguish the adapters from a careful reader of the
questionnaire (Figure 2, panel C; the online supplement tabulates every contrast).
Every interval covers zero, which the preregistration reads as “the adapters match a
careful reader”. That reading rests on few items. The coverage-matched contrasts use
the 5 items that the adapters and the face-valid rule both assign and the 9 items they
share with the typed decision model. The routes agree on dimension and sign for 1
and 7 of them. An interval that covers zero on so few items is consistent with a match
and does not establish one. Both readers concentrate on trust, where their own-item
A exceeds the 0.062 of the adapters’ own trust items, but their per-dimension cells
are too small to be informative.
Prompting the base model with the same six signs does not pass the registered
null. The same probe applied to the 64 prompted profiles gives A = 0.028 [−0.109,
0.158] with a one-sided permutation p of 0.509, and it assigns its selected items to a
single dimension, trust. The adapter route passes the null and the prompt route does
not, but the direct difference between them is unresolved. Adapter minus prompt
is 0.033 on each route’s own items and 0.004 on the 16 items both select, and both
intervals cover zero. The two routes agree on dimension and sign for 6 of those shared
items. We therefore cannot credit the trained weights over the prompt on the screen.
Item-level assignment is stable under option order and paraphrase, whereas membership in the particular selected set churns. The registered statistic is the number of

30

selected items whose dimension and sign are unchanged. It is 39 of 56 with reversed
options, 39 with the first paraphrase and 30 with the second. No variant changes more
than half, so the registered condition for wording dependence is not met, although the
second paraphrase comes close. Membership is another matter: the primary rule is a
top-quartile cut on |t|, and only 18, 29 and 16 of the 56 items are selected under both
the original readout and each variant. Dimension and direction are more stable than
the list of top-ranked items.

5.7

Instrument to respondents: order versus distance

The correlations above concern the order of countries. A different question is how far
the instrument’s response distribution lies from the survey’s. We measure it by the
country-equal mean total variation distance (TVD; lower is closer; D) in four cells.
They are the base model and the first-cohort adapters, each with and without the
country-named persona prompt.
The base model’s mean distance is 0.470. The adapters sit farther from the survey
at 0.515, and the persona prompt sits closer at 0.365 (panel B of Figure 4). A cell
mean carries no interval, whereas the paired contrasts of panel C each carry one.
Relative to the base model, the adapters are farther by 0.045 [0.006, 0.093] under
profile blocks, and the persona prompt is closer by −0.105 [−0.122, −0.086]. Country
resampling gives the same readings. By dimension, the adapters are farther than the

33

base model on four of the six dimensions and slightly closer on the other two (point
estimates; online supplement). With both interventions the distance is 0.420. Adding
the persona prompt to the adapters moves the distance by −0.096 [−0.115, −0.078],
and adding the adapters to the persona prompt moves it by 0.055 [0.016, 0.097]. The
interaction contrast is the adapters’ effect with the persona prompt minus their effect
without it. Under profile blocks it is 0.009 [−0.003, 0.022], an interval that includes
zero. Therefore, the data show no detectable departure from additivity.
The ordering of the cells by distance does not follow their ordering by trust
agreement with the GPS. The persona prompt has the lowest distance and no trust
order (Section 5.2). The adapters have the highest distance and keep the trust order.
Adding the persona prompt to the adapters keeps the trust order at 0.725 [0.505,
0.820] while the distance falls. The two measures ask different questions, and neither
says why the adapters’ distributions lie where they do. The instrument carries no
information about magnitudes (Section 6), so a distance to the survey’s response
distributions is not a quantity it was built to reduce.

6

Discussion

We asked a language model only about the group, and the answer comes in two parts
that point in opposite directions. Conditioned on declared group signs, the instrument
keeps the GPS order of countries on trust, also on a second scenario bank. Checked
against the Wave 7 respondents, its order of countries does not detectably agree with
theirs, and its response distributions sit farther from theirs than the base model’s
do. In other words, it can order countries the way a cross-national survey does while
answering unlike the people in them.

The conditioning method: what the signs carry. The declared sign carries the
trust order, whichever channel supplies it. The adapters keep the trust order across
scenario banks (Section 5.2). A country name does not recover that order, whereas
a prompt that states the six signs does. On trust the sign prompt correlates with
the GPS at 0.773 [0.584, 0.844], against 0.028 [−0.339, 0.381] for the named country.
In the screen, we could not resolve the contrast between the adapters and the sign
prompt. Therefore, the evidence cannot credit the trained weights over a prompt, and
the statement concerns trust only. The decomposition of the factorial shows where
trust differs from the other surfaces. Every own effect is positive, so the own part of
the main-effects prediction restates the correlation between the signs and the GPS on
every surface. What differs is how much the other five signs add. On trust they add
little, with Var(cross)/Var(full) at 0.147. On the other six surfaces they carry most of
the variation, with ratios from 0.830 to 1.049. The decomposition describes behavior
and leaves open why the other dimensions lose their order.
Training on sign-labeled pairs keeps the direction of each declared input and does
not deliver separate dimensions. Each sign moves its own surface in the intended
direction and also moves other surfaces. One common direction carries about half of
the variance across surfaces, and the planned separability check fails on both routes.
Prompting with the same signs is more entangled still, and three of its own effects
point the wrong way. Entanglement under intervention has also been reported when
cultural values are edited through a model’s internal activations (Dang and Masud,
2026). Our factorial adds a joint measurement for trained adapters and for a prompt
that carry the same declared information. We make no claim about the best training
recipe.

36

The evaluation protocol: what it separates. Each part of the protocol separated
something that a single agreement figure would have merged. Our second-bank check
turned four dimensions with a first-cohort signal into one. The screen’s permutation test
and its bootstrap interval answer different questions, and reporting both made the size
of the signal clearer than either would alone. Coverage-matched comparators showed
that the paired contrasts between the adapters and two readers of the questionnaire
are unresolved on the few items they share. The small overlap is part of that finding.
Notably, separating the three links showed that the GPS’s own agreement with the
human composite is loose on these items. Weak adapter–human agreement therefore
cannot be attributed to the instrument alone. It could arise from the instrument,
from the mapping between items and the GPS construct, or from limited coherence
between the two surveys. The design cannot tell these apart. The registered screen
and its freeze sequence are fixed before the human criterion is read, and we label the
descriptive analyses added afterwards as such. Another team can therefore apply the
protocol to a different conditioning method, anchor or criterion.

The failure profile: from instrument to respondents. Against respondents,
the retrospective screen returns a weak yes, and its signal sits on a dimension that
the second bank does not support (Section 5.4). The signal is a small fraction of the
two-wave human ceiling and is concentrated in risk taking. Trust, which the second
bank supports, does not stand apart from zero in the screen. We find no dimension
that passes both checks. Descriptively, the adapters’ order of countries does not
detectably agree with the respondents’ composite on any surface (Section 5.6).

Order and distribution. The split between order and distribution is what a grouplevel design gives us reason to expect. We chose to model the group directly and left

37

the aggregation over people to the GPS. An order of countries needs only a score
that rises with the group’s latent preference, and no representative respondent whose
answers equal the population’s (Kirman, 1992). A single choice policy per country,
however, has no spread within the country built in. That is, it can carry an order and
has no reason to match the distribution of respondents’ answers. We offer one reading
without testing it: the training signal states which of two responses is preferred and
says nothing about how strongly, or about how widely respondents spread across
the options. The order of countries is a quantity that signs speak to, whereas a
distance to the survey’s response distributions is one they carry no information about.
This argument explains why the adapters have no reason to approach the survey’s
response distributions. It does not explain why they move away from them, or why a
country-named prompt moves toward them.
The evidence shows the two targets crossing (Section 5.7). The adapters keep
the anchor’s order of countries on trust and lie farther from the survey’s response
distributions than the base model, by 0.045 [0.006, 0.093] in total variation distance.
A country-named prompt lies closer, by −0.105 [−0.122, −0.086], and carries no trust
order. If agreement were one quantity, the routes would fall in one ranking. They fall
in two. The contrast describes one anchor, one instrument family and one survey, and
it does not show that the country-named prompt matches respondents. A country
name has been found to recover a country ranking in the European Social Survey while
supporting no distributional inference (Wang, 2026). The separation of group-level
order from distributional fidelity is established (da Silva et al., 2026). Our case adds
one method whose two answers point in opposite directions, so order and distribution
have to be validated separately.

38

What a prospective validation would have to establish. A prospective validation would have to establish three things that the retrospective design cannot.
First, the probe’s selections and a human-only rule for choosing items would both be
declared before any new items are fielded. Second, the selected items would have to
separate countries in the assigned direction in new data. They would also have to
do so better than the human-only rule on matched countries and items. Third, the
dimension on which the signal appears would have to be one that the second bank
also supports. We would read a result of that kind as licensing the use before fielding.
Whether such a test would find the signal on trust, the one dimension the second
bank supports, is the open question this paper leaves.

Limits. Five limits apply to every result above. First, the human criterion is
retrospective and is the only human source. Registration and the freeze guard protect
against tuning the probe to it. They cannot make it independent of the survey program
from which the item pool was drawn. Second, we chose the country as the unit, and
our claims stay at that unit. The instrument is built from signs and cannot represent
magnitudes, differences among countries that share a sign profile, or variation among
people within a country. Country-level agreement does not establish individual-level
validity or measurement invariance (Robinson, 1950; Davidov et al., 2014; Bratt, 2025).
Third, all our adapters share one base model, one training recipe and one readout
convention. Their code probabilities have a dispersion that need not match human
response distributions. Fourth, the per-dimension cells are small and the selected set
churns under readout variants. Fifth, the noise checks are narrow. One unseeded
retrain is a narrow observed comparison for one unit, and two near-zero refits support
no claim across countries.

39

Intended use. Within those limits, the instrument has a modest and inspectable
use. A survey designer can read the dimension and direction it assigns to candidate
items as one input beside expert review and human pretesting, keeping in view that no
dimension passed both checks. The instrument’s value lies in what it makes explicit.
The declared moments, the sign rule and the absence of any group name at evaluation
are on the record, so a reader can see what information the instrument was given and
check where its readings depart from people. We see it as a complement to fielding
and pretesting, and the evidence gives no reason to replace them. In the terms of
Broska et al. (2025), its readings are potentially informative observations that still
require a human criterion.

7

Conclusion

Asked only about the group, a language model can order countries the way a crossnational survey does while answering unlike the people in them. We built synthetic
cultural agents that compile declared aggregate preference moments into choice policies
and read survey items without naming a group. We then tested each link of the chain
from anchor to instrument to respondents separately.
Between anchor and instrument, the method holds in a limited sense. Training
installs the synthetic labels on pairs the adapters never saw. On trust, the adapters
keep the anchor’s order of countries across a change of scenario bank. A country
name does not keep that order, and a prompt stating the same signs does. That is,
the declared signs carry the trust order, and the evidence cannot credit the trained
weights over a prompt. The adapters do not keep the declared dimensions separate.
Between instrument and respondents, the evidence is weak. A preregistered screen
of held-out items, read against a retrospective criterion, gives a small signal. The
40

registered comparisons do not separate it from careful readers of the questionnaire,
and no dimension passes both the second-bank check and the screen. Descriptively, the
adapters’ order of countries does not detectably agree with the respondents’ composite.
Their response distributions lie farther from the survey than the base model’s, whereas
a country-named prompt lies closer with no trust order. Keeping an order of countries
and matching respondents’ response distributions are different targets, and a design
built at the level of the group gives a reason to expect that they come apart.
We offer the method as an inspectable, group-level reading of candidate items that
a researcher may use beside human pretesting, as a complement and never in its place.
Our evaluation protocol applies to other conditioning methods and other anchors.
The test that would license more is prospective: probe-selected items fielded to people
after the selection and a human-only comparison rule have both been declared. Until
that test is run, pre-piloting remains untested.
